A stream cipher you can hold in your hands

Solitaire — Bruce Schneier's 1999 hand cipher, also called Pontifex — turns an ordinary deck of 54 playing cards into a keystream generator. Here is every step of it, running.

From solitaire-cipher-lab. The worked examples were recorded by that package's engine. Section 04 runs a browser port of it, checked against the original trace for trace.

Most explanations of stream ciphers ask you to imagine a keystream appearing from a black box. Solitaire doesn't need the black box. Its internal state is a deck of cards, and you can watch the state change.

This is a teaching cipher. Solitaire has known statistical biases — you can measure one yourself at the bottom of this page. Do not use it to protect real information.

Section 01The key is the deck

There is no password to remember and no number to key in. The order of the cards is the key. Two people who shuffle their decks into the same order can talk; anyone else is holding 54 factorial possible orderings, a number with 72 digits in it.

Every card has a value. Clubs are worth 1 to 13, diamonds 14 to 26, hearts 27 to 39, spades 40 to 52. Both jokers are worth 53 — which turns out to matter.

The deck in bridge order. This is the standard unkeyed starting position, used for test vectors — not a key.

Section 02One round, five steps

A round shuffles the deck in four fixed, entirely deterministic ways, then reads a single card to produce one keystream letter. No randomness is involved anywhere: the same deck always yields the same letters, which is exactly what lets the person at the other end reproduce them.

Step through it. Watch the two jokers wander down the deck, drag the cut points around with them, and eventually select a card.

Round four is worth stopping on. The count lands on a joker, and a joker has no letter — so the round is thrown away and run again. That discarded round is the single most commonly mis-implemented part of Solitaire, and it is invisible to any test that just encrypts and decrypts: a broken implementation happily decrypts its own broken ciphertext.

Why the triple cut is the interesting step

The two joker moves are small and local. The triple cut is the step that mixes globally: everything above the topmost joker and everything below the bottommost joker trade places wholesale, so a card near the top can be flung to the bottom in one move. The count cut then rotates the whole deck by an amount that depends on the card that happens to be sitting on the bottom. Together they make the next state depend on the whole deck, not just its neighbourhood.

Section 03From cards to letters

The cipher itself is almost an afterthought. Convert each plaintext letter to a number (A=1 … Z=26), add the keystream number, wrap at 26, convert back. That's it — the entire security of the scheme lives in the deck.

Decryption is the same operation with a minus sign. This symmetry is why the receiver needs an identically ordered deck and nothing else.

How should the deck be keyed?

Letters only; everything else is ignored. Both ends need the same phrase.

A real key is a shuffled deck, not a word. You would have to get this exact order to the other person somehow — which is the hard part of every cipher.

Ranks A 2-9 T J Q K, suits C D H S, plus JA and JB. — or type in the order of a deck you just shuffled by hand.

Section 05Never use the same deck twice

If you ran the section above twice, you have already seen this. Here is the failure written out: encrypt two messages with the same starting deck and an attacker can subtract one ciphertext from the other. The keystream — the thing you were protecting — cancels out completely.






The attacker never learns the key and does not need to. They learn the difference between your two plaintexts, and with any guess about one message, they get the other. This is not a flaw in Solitaire specifically; it is true of every additive stream cipher. Solitaire just makes it visible.



Section 06It looks random. It isn't.



The keystream above looks like noise. Here is the test that says otherwise, and you can run it yourself in a few seconds.



In an ideal stream over 26 letters, the chance that any letter equals the one before it is 1 in 26 — about 3.85%. Solitaire repeats more often than that:











That gap is small, consistent, and fatal to the claim that the output is indistinguishable from random — which is the property a stream cipher is supposed to have. The bias was spotted by Paul Crowley in 1999, shortly after the cipher was published, and analysed in detail by Daniel Shiu in 2019. Crowley also showed that the deck transformation is not reversible, although the original description says it is: a joker on the bottom wraps to second from the top, which is exactly where a joker on the top lands too, so two different decks can step to the same next one. That does not stop ordinary encryption and decryption working, since both sides generate the keystream forwards.



The lesson is not "Solitaire is broken, use something else." It is that looking scrambled and being unpredictable are different claims, and that the distance between them is measured, not argued. Modern stream ciphers are designed from the start so that no statistic like this one separates their output from noise — and they are published precisely so that people can go looking.
